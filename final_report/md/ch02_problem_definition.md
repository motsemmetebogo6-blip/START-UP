# Background and context

High-carbon ferrochrome is produced by melting chromite ore with carbon-rich reductants in a submerged arc furnace, a process in which electricity is both the heat source and one of the largest operating costs (Gasik, 2013). South African producers have operated for years under rising electricity tariffs: approved increases of 12.74 % took effect in April 2025 and a further 8.76 % in April 2026 (Eskom, 2026), while the selling price of the alloy is set in US dollars on international markets over which the producer has no control (SMM, 2026). The practical consequence for any producer is that competitiveness must come from the furnace itself: more tonnes of alloy from the same electrical installation, at lower energy per tonne.

The furnace in this study is a closed, three-phase submerged arc furnace supplied through transformers rated at 65 MVA (Ferrochrome Data Workbook, 2026). Management intends to raise its average electrical power input from approximately 55 MW to approximately 60 MW, in order to increase production without compromising furnace stability, electrode performance, refractory life, metallurgical recovery or product quality.

# The current state of the furnace

The plant's own data establish the present operating point (Ferrochrome Data Workbook, 2026). The furnace produces 393.65 t/d of charge-grade ferrochrome (50.4 % Cr) at a specific energy consumption of 3.5 MWh/t, drawing a mean power of 57.43 MW, the product of the 65 MVA transformer rating, an assumed power factor of 0.93 and a calibrated load factor of 0.95. The plant is therefore already operating at the full capability of its transformers when on load; it is power-limited, not capacity-limited. The difference between the brief's "approximately 55 MW" and the data's 57.43 MW is believed to reflect electrical losses between the transformers and the furnace bath (an implied secondary-circuit efficiency of 0.958).

A consistency check on the plant data adds one metallurgical finding: the chromium recovery assumed in the data (78 %) cannot be reconciled with an oxide-tracer balance on the measured ore and slag analyses, which places recovery at about 87-88 %, with corresponding ore consumption of 2.13 t/t alloy rather than the 2.39 t/t implied by the assumed recovery. The true recovery must therefore be established before the value of any improvement can be quantified. The full mass balance in Chapter 8 confirms a recovery of 86.9 %.

# Statement of the problem

The problem is stated as follows:

How can the existing closed, three-phase ferrochrome submerged arc furnace be uprated from its present operating power to 60 MW mean power on its existing 65 MVA transformer installation, while retaining the existing furnace infrastructure and maintaining safe, stable and efficient metallurgical operation, and can that uprate deliver the production target of at least 450 t/d?

The difficulty inside this question is mathematical as much as metallurgical. At a fixed transformer rating,

::: {custom-style="Equation"}
mean power = rating × power factor × load factor,
:::

and daily production equals the daily energy divided by the specific energy. Production can therefore be raised only by improving the power factor, the load factor or the specific energy, and the analysis of the existing condition shows that the production target of 450 t/d would require 65.6 MW mean at the present 3.5 MWh/t, which exceeds even the maximum the transformers can deliver while on load. No purely electrical adjustment can meet both requirements at once; the specific energy must also fall. This is the central problem the project must solve.

# Constraints within which the problem must be solved

Four constraints bound any solution.

* The transformer rating is fixed at 65 MVA, and the power factor may not exceed 0.95.
* The furnace shell, hearth and roof are retained: the project is a modification of an operating plant, not a new build, and in such brownfield work the feasibility of an uprate is decided by the most constrained component in the system, which must be identified explicitly (Mulholland, 2009).
* The slag operates with only about 44 °C of superheat above its liquidus, so any modification that shifts heat away from the lower furnace (such as raising the electrode tips) risks slag freezing and tapping failure.
* The auxiliary systems (cooling, off-gas cleaning, feed and tapping) may be modified only where the additional duty justifies it (Chapter 4).

# What the problem is not

For clarity, the problem is explicitly not: the purchase of a larger transformer (the 65 MVA rating is fixed), the replacement or resizing of the electrodes, which operate within normal current-density ranges at 60 MW, or the installation of grid-side power-factor correction, which improves the power factor seen by the utility but cannot release additional capacity inside the furnace transformers.

# Success criteria

A solution is successful only if it satisfies the acceptance criteria of the design basis: apparent power within 65 MVA continuously, power factor between 0.93 and 0.95, production of at least 450 t/d, alloy quality within present ranges with positive slag superheat, chromium recovery not below the baseline established by the mass balance, specific energy not above 3.5 MWh/t, and a positive net present value.
